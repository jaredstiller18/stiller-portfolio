1 of 7 — Tan Lab, CU Boulder

Closed-Loop Hypoxic Air Delivery and Control System

Project Objective

Build a closed-loop rig that uses a Hypoxico altitude training system to automatically hold a subject's blood oxygen saturation within a target band by switching between hypoxic and normoxic air. The rig is custom-built for physiology research, not a commercial kit.

Process & Skills

  • Administrative: Formatted a BOM for approval, ordered parts, and filed the appropriate paperwork (tax refunds). Later produced a standard operating procedure (SOP) and system architecture documentation.
  • System architecture: Designed a dual-stream gas delivery system that switches between a Hypoxico HYP-123 hypoxic generator and a variable-speed normoxic blower, with upstream reservoir bags, 22 mm CPAP tubing, and a Y-connector merging both streams to the subject's mask.
  • Valve control hardware: Built a four-solenoid valve manifold (2-way, NPT) driven by a USB relay board (FTDI FT245R) in asynchronous bitbang mode, with a fail-safe default that routes normoxic air to the mask when all valves are de-energized.
  • Safety: Wired a normally closed emergency stop in series with the valve power line and added a software cutoff that forces normoxic air if SpO2 drops below 75%, holding it until SpO2 recovers to 82%.
  • Instrumentation interface: Wrote a Python serial reader for a Masimo Root monitor (921600 baud, ASCII protocol) that parses SpO2, heart rate, perfusion index, and respiratory rate at 1 Hz in a background thread and computes the real-time SpO2 trend by linear regression.
  • Control algorithm: Developed a lag-compensated predictive controller that holds SpO2 at 80% by keeping the subject on hypoxic air and delivering brief, adaptive normoxic pulses at a 78% floor, with early-exit, trend-based abort, and hard-cap logic to prevent overshoot.
  • System identification: Characterized desaturation rate (~0.38%/s), resaturation rate (~5%/s peak), and valve-to-SpO2 lag (~9 s mean) across 18 sessions, then used these measurements to tune pulse timing, trend windows, and minimum dwell times, tightening control to 79–83%.
  • Dashboard and logging: Built a Plotly Dash dashboard with live SpO2 charting, valve-switch markers, manual overrides, and a selector between controller variants, plus CSV logging that timestamps every valve state change and event.
  • Debugging and reliability: Resolved a USB enumeration conflict between two FTDI devices with identical VID/PID by identifying the relay through serial-number exclusion. Also added relay auto-reconnect and 30-second health checks, and fixed a Dash callback batching bug with per-button callbacks.
  • Design iteration: Developed an alternate bag-downstream architecture and a matching controller with longer pulses, lag constants, and dwell times to produce smoother FiO2 transitions.
PID control Solenoid/relay driving Serial protocol integration Safety-first design

2 of 7 — Tan Lab, CU Boulder

nRF52 BLE Research Instrumentation Platform

Project Objective

Unify three separate pieces of physiology research hardware (a NIRS monitor, a pneumatic cuff inflator, and an electrical stimulator) under one BLE bridge and web dashboard. The device lets the lab run a single sequenced protocol with shared timestamps rather than juggling three standalone instruments.

Process & Skills

  • Circuit design: Designed a relay driver circuit and a stimulator trigger circuit to control pneumatic cuff inflation and neuromuscular electrical stimulation via the nRF52's GPIOs.
  • Administrative: Formatted a BOM for approval, ordered parts, and filed the appropriate paperwork (tax refunds). Later produced a standard operating procedure (SOP) and system architecture documentation.
  • Hardware construction: Hand-built and soldered the circuits above, then housed the complete device in an enclosure with a Micro-USB output to the computer, a BNC output to the e-stim, and a 1/4" audio jack to the cuff inflator.
  • Embedded firmware: Wrote the BLE bridge in Zephyr RTOS (C89, NCS v2.0.0) using the Nordic nRF52-DK to stream data over BLE from a Moxy near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) monitor.
  • Protocol engineering: Implemented the specified research protocol for cuff inflation and precise 5 V TTL triggering (~10 ms) for the DS7R stimulator.
  • Full-stack dashboard: Built the live monitoring and protocol-runner interface in vanilla JS, Chart.js, and Tailwind, with the entire protocol runnable from one button. Added contingency manual controls for the cuff inflator and e-stim, plus a manual save button on top of automatic hard-drive backups.
Zephyr / C firmware BLE Analog driver design Protocol sequencing

3 of 7 — MCEN 5055: Advanced Product Design

Automated Parking Brake for a Wheeled Walker

Project Objective

Design an automated parking brake that reduces the cognitive and physical load on walker users.

Process & Skills

  • Field research: Conducted in-person and virtual interviews with walker users, nurses, and physicians at local retirement homes to identify their needs. Returned throughout the prototyping stage for continuous user feedback as the concept developed.
  • Ideation: Brainstormed comprehensively using concept maps, customer journey maps, and affinity diagrams.
  • Administrative and website design: Submitted a BOM for ordering. Documented the design process (need-finding, ideation, prototyping, reflection, and marketing) on a website platform.
  • Circuit and hardware design: Designed a capacitive brake-trigger circuit using copper tape formed to the walker handles as capacitive sensors. An Arduino Nano processed the touch signal and drove the brake actuators. Electronics, drivers, and actuators ran off a portable power source connected to the walker.
  • Hardware construction: Hand-built and soldered the circuit above. Integrated linear actuators into the walker's braking system and a capacitive sensing system in the handles — all fixed to the walker while retaining its foldability and portability.
  • Software design: Wrote C/C++ code for a touch-threshold brake response that engages the parking brake when the user loses stable contact with the handle sensor array, refining it through iterative, observed field tests.
Capacitive sensing Arduino / C++ Actuator integration User research

6 of 7 — Thesis Project: Giovanetti Lab, Williams College

PMMA Barrel Temperature Monitoring System

Project Objective

Build a temperature-monitoring subsystem for a full-scale mockup of the DarkSide-20K dark matter detector's cryogenic core. The system protects the time projection chamber's (TPC's) PMMA structure from thermal-shock cracking during cooldown.

Process & Skills

  • Finite element analysis: Used FEA to determine capsule sizing for slip-fit interference, using material properties to secure the sensor when cooled.
  • Bill of materials and wiring diagrams: Developed a BOM for wiring 44 RTDs out of the cryogenic chamber, through a flange, into analog-to-digital converters, then to a central platform.
  • Prototyping: Performed mechanical stress testing on different RTD-capsule-epoxy configurations to determine the best fit for insertion.
  • FEA verification: Performed liquid-nitrogen tests on capsules embedded in PMMA to verify FEA simulations and PMMA safety.
  • Instrumentation: Instrumented 44 RTDs with 3-wire readout across different panels and depths of the PMMA barrel.
  • Software: Implemented a 3-wire resistance readout into ADCs for each sensor.
  • Dashboard output: Built a live Grafana dashboard for real-time thermal-gradient monitoring during cooling.

Results

Sensor readout held within 1 °C agreement across the sensor array during live cryostat cooldown — sufficient precision to inform a controlled cooling protocol.

RTD / ADC design Cryogenic instrumentation Thermal monitoring

Robotics & Controls: Challenge Problems

Project Objective

Results

  • CP2: Achieved a 90% goal-reach rate across 100 randomized trials.
  • CP3: Wrote code that accurately segments JPG images and redraws them with a virtual robotic arm using inverse kinematics (see video).
  • CP4: Stabilized the inverted pendulum and produced a clean step response with the tuned PD controller.
